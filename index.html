Spinning the Numbers: 608 Days of America’s Spotify Top 200

Jewlya Arrington · Financial Data Analytics · Data: Spotify Daily Top Songs USA,

This report follows every song on Spotify’s daily Top 200 chart for the United States from January 1, 2025 to August 31, 2026: 608 days, 121,594 chart spots and 60.6 billion streams. The chart looks crowded, with 2,389 different songs passing through it, but a small group of artists and songs earns an outsized share of the streams. About half of all songs chart for a week or less, while a handful stay for months or the whole period. New music arrives mostly on Fridays, songs that debut near the top last far longer than the rest, and older hits are taking a growing share of what people stream.

Track 01 · Artists

Ten artists earned a quarter of all Top-200 streams

Out of 608 lead artists who reached the US Top 200, just ten collected 25.3% of all streams on the chart. Morgan Wallen led by a wide margin with 2.86 billion streams, or 4.7% of the total, about half again as much as Bad Bunny (3.2%) and Kendrick Lamar (3.1%) in second and third. The top six artists, the top 1% of the list, took 18.5% on their own.

If every record in the grid stands for 1% of all streams, Morgan Wallen owns five of them and each of the other top-ten artists owns two or three. The cut at ten is only where the list stops: fifteen more artists, led by Tyler, The Creator (1.56%, almost level with Olivia Dean at 1.58%), also reached 1% and get records of their own. The other 583 artists together make up the last 56 records. Reaching the chart is common; staying on it and piling up streams is concentrated in a few careers.

    Track 02 · Songs

    A hundred songs earned more than 40% of all streams

    The same pattern holds for songs. Of the 2,389 songs that charted, the top 10 earned 7.0% of all streams and the top 100, only about 4% of the songs, earned 42.8%. By the top 500 the total reaches 87.1%, which leaves nearly 1,900 songs to split the remaining 13%.

    The most-streamed song was Alex Warren’s “Ordinary” with 472.5 million US streams across two versions, just ahead of Kendrick Lamar and SZA’s “luther” (459.4 million) and Bad Bunny’s “DtMF” (451.6 million). The dashed line shows what an even split would look like; the gap between it and the real curve is how concentrated the chart is.

      Track 03 · #1 spot

      Most #1 songs held the top spot for days, a few held it for months

      Fifty-two different songs reached #1 during the 608 days, but most did not stay long: the typical #1 song spent 3.5 days there in total, and 16 songs held the spot for a single day. The top spot was held in 111 separate runs.

      A few songs dominated anyway. Ella Langley’s “Choosin’ Texas” spent 104 days at #1 across two versions, more than any other song, followed by “Golden” from KPop Demon Hunters (71 days) and Taylor Swift’s “The Fate of Ophelia” (56 days). The longest unbroken runs were 30 straight days, a tie between “luther” (February 12 to March 13, 2025) and, from the holidays, Brenda Lee’s “Rockin’ Around the Christmas Tree” (November 27 to December 26, 2025).

        Track 04 · Lifespan

        Half of all songs lasted a week or less

        Charting is usually brief. The median song spent just 7 days on the US Top 200, 51.5% of songs charted for a week or less, and 23.2% charted for exactly one day. Only 27.3% lasted a month or more, and just 3.7%, or 89 songs, stayed a year or more.

        At the other extreme, five songs were on the chart all 608 days: “Sailor Song” by Gigi Perez (across two versions), “505” by Arctic Monkeys, “Mr. Brightside” by The Killers, “Sweater Weather” by The Neighbourhood and “No Pole” by Don Toliver. The average run (50.9 days) is far above the median because of long-running songs like these.

          Track 05 · Debuts

          Songs that debut in the top 10 last far longer

          Where a song enters the chart says a lot about how long it stays. Songs that debuted in the top 10 lasted a median of 29 days, compared with 8 days for songs debuting at #11–50 and only 2 days for songs debuting below #50.

          The first 60 days show the difference. Among top-10 debuts with a full 60 days of data, 98% were still on the chart on their seventh day, 51% on day 30 and 35% on day 60. Among songs that debuted at #11–50, those figures drop to 66%, 13% and 8%. Most debuts fall fast: 77% of songs that debuted in this period had their biggest streaming day on their very first day. The ones that start near the top are the ones that hold on.

            Track 06 · Collaborations

            Collaborations perform about the same as solo songs

            About one in four charting songs was a collaboration (24.5%), and collaborations earned a similar share of streams (20.6%). Per chart spot, collaborations averaged 516,744 streams a day compared with 493,642 for solo songs, only about 5% more.

            Collaborations did not last longer either: the median collaboration charted for 6 days and the median solo song for 7, and their best chart positions were nearly identical (#65 and #64). In this data, having more than one credited artist makes little difference to how a song performs once it is on the chart.

              Track 07 · Release day

              Friday is release day

              New music arrives on Fridays. Of the 1,626 songs that debuted during these 608 days, 1,178, or 72.4%, first appeared on a Friday’s chart. No other day accounts for more than 7% of debuts, which is why Fridays form the bright stripe in the calendar when you switch it to debuts.

              Listening follows the releases. Fridays averaged 109.4 million Top-200 streams, compared with 90.9 million on Sundays, the quietest day, about 20% more. The single biggest day in the data was Christmas Day 2025, with 238.8 million streams, about 2.4 times an average day. Holiday songs, led by “Rockin’ Around the Christmas Tree,” took all ten top spots and 95.6% of that day’s streams.

                Track 08 · Catalog

                Older hits are taking a growing share of streams

                Songs that had already been on the chart for more than a year earned 30.4% of all streams in the period. That share grew over time, from 23.7% in January 2025 to 38.6% in August 2026, with ups and downs from month to month.

                Their share comes from staying power, not from bigger days: catalog songs held 34.6% of all chart spots but averaged fewer streams per spot than current songs (438,432 compared with 529,845 a day). As long-running hits stay on the chart, newer songs have to compete with them for the same 200 positions. Note that “catalog” here means time on the chart, not how old the song is.

                  Liner notes: about the data

                  Everything on this page comes from one source and a few simple rules, listed here so every number can be checked and rebuilt.

                  Source
                  Spotify Charts (charts.spotify.com), “Daily Top Songs USA.” One CSV per day, downloaded for every date from to ( days).
                  One row
                  One Spotify track (one version of a song) on the US Top 200 on one date — rows in total.
                  Rows dropped
                  None. Every day has 200 rows except 2026-07-13 to 2026-07-18, which have 199 because Spotify’s own chart skips one position on each of those days.
                  Streams
                  US streams counted by Spotify for songs on the Top 200 that day. Songs outside the Top 200 are not included, so totals are Top-200 totals, not all US streaming.
                  Share
                  A group’s streams divided by all Top-200 streams in the period (or in the month, for monthly shares).
                  Days charted
                  The number of dates a song (any version) appears in this data (at most ). Songs that were already charting before 2025 or still charting after August 2026 are cut off at the edges.
                  Lead artist
                  The first credited artist. “Tyler, The Creator” and “Earth, Wind & Fire” are kept as single names.
                  Song
                  Spotify sometimes lists the same song under two or three track IDs (re-releases, deluxe or edited versions). Versions with the same title and the same lead artist are counted as one song, with their streams and chart days combined: track IDs become songs ( songs had more than one version). Where it matters, the report says “across N versions.” A song’s name, artists and solo/collaboration type come from its most-streamed version.
                  Collaboration
                  A song whose most-streamed version credits more than one artist.
                  Debut
                  A song’s first day in the data when it is also its first day ever on the chart (Spotify’s days-on-chart count is 1). A new version of a song that already charted is not a debut. The 60-day paths in finding 5 use only songs that debuted in the top 50 on or before July 3, 2026, so each has 60 days of data.
                  Holiday songs
                  Songs whose chart days all fall in November, December or January.
                  Catalog
                  On a given date, a chart spot whose track has been on the chart for more than 365 days, using Spotify’s own days-on-chart count for that version (which includes days before 2025). This measures time on the chart, not release date.
                  Streams per chart spot
                  Total streams divided by the number of chart rows (one row = one song on one day).
                  Average daily streams
                  For the weekday chart: total Top-200 streams on each date, averaged over all dates that fall on that weekday.
                  Catalog share
                  Streams from catalog songs in a month divided by all Top-200 streams in that month.
                  Median vs. average
                  Medians are used for days charted because a few very long runs pull the average up.
                  Reproduce
                  python3 scripts/build_data.py then python3 scripts/analysis.py rebuilds every number on this page (data/report.json).
